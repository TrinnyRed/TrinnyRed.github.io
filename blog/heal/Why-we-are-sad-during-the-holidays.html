Why We Are Sad During the Holidays, Even Though It's Supposed to Be a Happy Time

Nov 20, 2026

There’s a compelling myth that I heard from a family member from law enforcement: suicide rates spike during the holiday season. The truth is, it’s actually the lowest in December. (The highest is spring and summer for certain physiological and social reasons).

All the same, high suicide rates during the Holidays are a persistent myth.

I believed it because I feel more melancholy during the end-of-year family-gathering times than any other time of the year.

Some statistics that show we’re not alone:

Metric Statistic Source
Worsening mental health 64% of people with mental health conditions say the holidays make their symptoms worse. National Alliance on Mental Illness (NAMI)
Elevated holiday stress 89% of U.S. adults say the season causes stress; 41% report significantly higher stress than during other times of the year. American Psychological Association (APA)
Pressure to perform “happiness” 69% of adults feel pressure to act happier than they actually feel. APA
Loneliness & isolation 66% feel lonely during the season, even though about one in four of that 66% are able to be with their loved ones. NAMI
Top emotional triggers 58% cite financial strain, 40–48% cite grief or missing loved ones, and 32–36% cite family conflict or feeling like the holidays are a competition. APA
January therapy surge Mental health appointment bookings drop 11% in Nov–Dec (as people push through the season) and jump 26% in January. Zocdoc Healthcare Data

While the suicide rate is a myth, there is also a post-holiday rebound: The LGBTQ+ youth crisis organization The Trevor Project found that crisis outreach drops 20-40% on Thanksgiving, Christmas, and New Year’s. But there’s an uptick in crisis contacts two days immediately following the holidays.

General mortality studies from CDC/APPC also found a higher risk of suicide on New Year’s Day specifically.

Why?

Why do we feel sad, stressed, or lonely during the holidays, when we expect it to be cozy, uplifting, and soul-nourishing?

Why does being surrounded by people make us feel more alone than when we weren’t surrounded?

Ultimately, it comes down to how we want to feel safe, loved, and known, by the very people who raised us and who have been with us since the beginning.

It comes down to wanting to feel like we belong, like this is our tribe, and that if bad things ever happened, these are the people we could rely on.

It comes down to wanting to feel like we had a good year, and these holidays are celebrations for making it.

We’re fine the rest of the year because there’s no Hallmark vision of love and care. But when we’re planning the difficult Thanksgiving menu, or awkwardly sitting next to our parents on Christmas, or getting drunk on New Year’s secretly feeling like we’re running out of time rather than grateful, the social expectations and our own internal realities stand in stark contrast.

And that’s OK.

We don’t have to compound our sadness and pain with guilt that we’re not living up to the holiday expectations.

There may be a gap we’re forced to confront yearly – a gap between society’s festive, sentimental mood, and our own feelings about our own family or this past year.

No, we can’t rely on our family. We’ve had to struggle so much on our own, all this time. Many families out there are enjoying the holidays with theirs, having had a good, productive year and looking forward to another one, while we feel confused, upset, and sad about our own shortcomings.

It’s OK to admit all that.

It doesn’t have to sour the holidays. It doesn’t mean we’re ungrateful.

It just means we’re being a little more honest and brave.

And that’s a whole lot more meaningful and right than pretending otherwise.


If you or someone you know is struggling or in crisis, help is available. You can call or text 988 or chat at 988lifeline.org to reach the Suicide & Crisis Lifeline—free, confidential, and available 24/7.
